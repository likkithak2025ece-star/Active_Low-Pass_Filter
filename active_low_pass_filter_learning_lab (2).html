Active Low-Pass Filter

A sensor signal may contain high-frequency noise. This learning lab explains how an op-amp active low-pass filter passes low-frequency signals while attenuating higher-frequency components, using the supplied Multisim circuit and Bode plot.

1. Introduction and learning objectives

An active low-pass filter (LPF) is a frequency-selective circuit that passes signals below a chosen cutoff frequency and reduces signals above it. An op-amp plus resistors and a capacitor can provide filtering and voltage gain without requiring an inductor.

By the end, you can…Identify the inverting active LPF topology, calculate its cutoff frequency and passband gain, and interpret its frequency response.
PrerequisitesOhm’s law, impedance of a capacitor, ideal op-amp rules, complex frequency response, and decibels.
Core terminologyPassband, stopband, cutoff frequency, pole, gain, roll-off, phase shift, and bandwidth.

Why it matters

Sensor outputs often include electrical interference and switching noise. Filtering before an ADC or controller can reduce unwanted high-frequency content, provided the desired signal lies within the passband.

Common types

TypeTypical feature
Passive LPFUses R, C (and possibly L); cannot provide active voltage gain.
Active first-order LPFUses an amplifier and RC network; one pole, nominal roll-off −20 dB/decade.
Higher-order active LPFMultiple poles; sharper attenuation, with more design sensitivity.

3. Working principle

Low-frequency operation

At low frequencies, C1 has high impedance, so R2 dominates the feedback path. The circuit behaves approximately as an inverting amplifier with gain −R2/R1.

At the cutoff frequency

At \(f_c\), the output magnitude is 0.707 of the passband magnitude (−3.01 dB). The capacitor and feedback resistor have equal impedance magnitudes: \(X_C=R_2\).

High-frequency operation

As frequency increases, capacitor impedance decreases. The feedback impedance magnitude falls, so the closed-loop gain decreases. For a first-order filter, the asymptotic roll-off is −20 dB per decade (about −6 dB per octave).

Capacitor impedance: ZC = 1 / (jωC), where ω = 2πf

Ideal assumptions and limitations

  • Ideal op-amp approximation: input current is approximately zero and negative feedback holds the input differential voltage near zero.
  • The real op-amp has finite gain-bandwidth, slew rate, input noise, offset, and output swing limits.
  • The filter attenuates frequencies, not “noise” by label; wanted signal components above the cutoff are also reduced.
  • Check that the op-amp remains stable with the chosen capacitive feedback and that output loading is reasonable.

4. Design equations and calculations

Transfer function

The feedback impedance is the parallel combination of R2 and C1:

Zf = R2 || 1/(sC) = R2 / (1 + sR2C)
H(s) = Vout/Vin = −Zf/R1 = −(R2/R1) / (1 + sR2C)

Therefore, the passband gain magnitude is \(A_0=R_2/R_1\), and the cutoff frequency is:

fc = 1 / (2π R2 C1)

At the given input frequency of 1 kHz, the ideal gain magnitude is:

|H(jω)| = (R2/R1) / √[1 + (f/fc)²] ≈ 1 / √[1 + (1000/1591.5)²] ≈ 0.847
Vout,peak ≈ 1 V × 0.847 = 0.847 V peak (ideal, unloaded)

The output is inverted. Its phase is approximately −180° − tan⁻¹(f/fc) using a continuous phase convention (equivalently +180° − tan⁻¹(f/fc) modulo 360°).

6. Solved numerical examples

Example 1 — Find cutoff frequency

Given: R2 = 10 kΩ, C = 10 nF. Find: \(f_c\).

fc = 1/(2πRC) = 1/[2π × 10⁴ × 10⁻⁸] = 1.59 kHz

Answer: Approximately 1.59 kHz. Frequencies below this are passed with relatively little attenuation.

Example 2 — Gain and output amplitude at 1 kHz

Given: R1 = R2 = 10 kΩ, C = 10 nF, Vin = 1 V peak, f = 1 kHz.

A0 = −R2/R1 = −1
|H| = 1/√[1+(1000/1591.5)²] = 0.847
Vout,peak ≈ 0.847 × 1 V = 0.847 V

Answer: About 0.847 V peak, with inversion and additional frequency-dependent phase lag.

Example 3 — Choose C for a 500 Hz cutoff

Given: R2 = 10 kΩ, desired \(f_c=500\) Hz.

C = 1/(2πR2fc) = 1/[2π × 10,000 × 500] ≈ 31.8 nF

Answer: Use approximately 31.8 nF (a standard 33 nF value gives a cutoff near 482 Hz).

GATE-style calculation shortcut

For a first-order LPF, the corner depends on the RC product. Doubling R2 or C halves \(f_c\). At exactly \(f_c\), magnitude is \(A_0/\sqrt2\), not \(A_0/2\).

8. Core electronics interview questions

Expand each question to review a concise answer and practical follow-up point.

1. What is an active low-pass filter?

A frequency-selective circuit using an active device such as an op-amp with passive components. It passes low-frequency components and attenuates high-frequency components.

2. Why use an op-amp instead of only an RC network?

An op-amp can provide gain and high input impedance, and can isolate stages. It requires power and has bandwidth, noise, slew-rate, and output-swing limits.

3. What determines the cutoff frequency in this circuit?

The feedback resistor R2 and capacitor C1: \(f_c=1/(2πR_2C_1)\). R1 sets the passband gain along with R2.

4. Why is the output inverted?

The input signal is applied to the inverting terminal through R1, while the non-inverting terminal is grounded. Negative feedback makes the closed-loop gain negative.

5. What does −3 dB mean?

It means voltage magnitude is about 0.707 of the reference magnitude when impedances are comparable; power is half the reference power.

6. What roll-off do you expect from a first-order filter?

Approximately −20 dB/decade, or −6 dB/octave, well above the pole frequency.

7. How would you move the cutoff to a lower frequency?

Increase R2 or C1 because cutoff is inversely proportional to their product. Recheck gain and practical op-amp limitations when changing R2.

8. The measured cutoff differs from theory. What do you check?

Component values/tolerances, feedback wiring, source and load impedance, sweep setup, op-amp model and supply rails, and whether the measured level is 3 dB below the actual passband.

9. Why might the output waveform clip?

The required output may exceed the op-amp's output swing, or the input/common-mode range or supply wiring may be incorrect. Check amplitude and power rails.

10. What practical limitations matter before an ADC?

Choose cutoff relative to the desired signal bandwidth, consider ADC sampling and anti-aliasing requirements, noise, tolerance, op-amp bandwidth, output drive, and recovery from overload.

11. Does a low-pass filter remove all noise?

No. It attenuates frequency components above its passband; noise inside the passband remains, and wanted high-frequency content is also attenuated.

12. How can you verify the design in the lab?

Apply a known sine wave, sweep frequency, measure Vin and Vout, compute gain in dB, and identify the point 3 dB below passband gain. Also inspect time-domain phase and amplitude.

9. Applications and industry relevance

Sensor conditioningReduce high-frequency interference on temperature, pressure, vibration, or light-sensor signals before conversion.
Audio circuitsRemove unwanted high-frequency components in tone-control or signal-conditioning stages.
Embedded systemsAnalog anti-alias filtering before ADC sampling; the cutoff must be designed around the wanted signal and sampling rate.
InstrumentationCondition low-bandwidth measurement signals and reduce out-of-band disturbance.

Engineering trade-offs

  • Higher-order filters offer steeper attenuation but require more components and careful stability/tolerance analysis.
  • Component tolerances shift the cutoff frequency.
  • Op-amp gain-bandwidth product should comfortably exceed the required closed-loop operating bandwidth.
  • A filter is not a substitute for good grounding, shielding, layout, and sensor wiring.

Common errors to avoid

  • Using R1 instead of R2 in the cutoff equation for this topology.
  • Forgetting that the op-amp is configured as an inverting amplifier.
  • Confusing 1 V peak with 1 V peak-to-peak or RMS.
  • Calling the cutoff the point where output becomes zero; a first-order filter has no abrupt cutoff.
  • Reporting the screenshot as a precise measurement when no cursor reading is shown.
